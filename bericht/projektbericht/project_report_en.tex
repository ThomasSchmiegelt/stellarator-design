\noindent{\Large\bfseries Introduction and motivation}\par\medskip
I am not a fusion researcher. This is a private project, driven by curiosity: how does a stellarator work, and
how far can one person get with open software and AI assistance on such a demanding part? Stellarator coils are a
good example because design, manufacturing and strength meet here. Physics dictates the path, current and field
create the forces, and the structure has to fit in between. Later the plasma vessel was added, with a cooling
core of gyroid filaments.

A stellarator confines a fusion plasma with twisted 3D superconducting coils that carry large magnetic forces.
In four days, a human and two AI agents built an end-to-end pipeline from a public coil dataset to a
structural analysis of the coil casings: coil path $\to$ magnetic field $\to$ Lorentz force $\to$ casing
geometry $\to$ stress. This report is mostly about \emph{how} it was built: the division of labour between a local
agent (Hermes) and a coding agent (Claude Code), a first model that was wrong while looking right, a fully
parametric CAD model that broke the mesher, and a second, CAD-free code path that finally worked. For a
W7-X-like system of 70 coils the result is 99\,\% of casing stresses below 43\,MPa -- under strongly simplified
supports. A follow-up (Section~\ref{sec:cooling}) computes the flow through the gyroid cooling core of the
1\,GW plasma vessel with FluidX3D and suggests improvements.

\section{Setup}
\kasten{Stellarator in five sentences.}{A fusion plasma (over 100 million degrees) is held by a magnetic field
inside a ring-shaped vacuum vessel. To keep particles from drifting out, field lines must wind helically around
the ring. A tokamak gets this twist from a large current in the plasma itself; a stellarator gets it entirely
from external coils, which therefore have to be twisted in 3D. This makes steady-state operation easier but turns
each coil into a demanding 3D part with tight tolerances. The reference device is Wendelstein~7-X (IPP
Greifswald) with 50 non-planar and 20 planar superconducting coils \cite{bosch2013}.}

The agents never talk directly. They coordinate through files in the repository: an append-only findings log
(\texttt{BEFUNDE.md}; every entry has observation, measurement, location, status), a handover file for decisions
and running jobs (\texttt{ERINNERUNGEN.md}), and skills -- short Markdown playbooks an agent loads before a task.
Long simulations are started detached (\texttt{setsid nohup}) so they survive a session ending or a context
compaction. The very first human intervention was a stop: Hermes started building tooling right away and was told
to do the research first.

\section{Research phase}
Hermes collected the basics: W7-X has a major radius of 5.5\,m, five field periods and 2.5\,T on axis
\cite{bosch2013}. Each coil is wound from a cable-in-conduit conductor: 243 NbTi strands of 0.57\,mm, each with
144 filaments in copper, inside a co-extruded 16\,mm $\times$ 16\,mm aluminium jacket with 37\,\% void for
helium cooling \cite{rummel2005,maix2006}. From a set of 33 PDFs supplied by the user, four were relevant; the most
useful one sizes coil structures for EU-DEMO with a 660\,MPa stress limit and wedged inner legs
\cite{bachmann2024}. A rate-limited arXiv search added work on HTS strain sensitivity \cite{schmeling2025} and on
manufacturing tolerances (W7-X: 2\,mm) \cite{gil2025}.

\kasten{Lesson -- verify citations.}{The bibliography an agent produced during research contained entries
without authors and with wrong venues or years. For this report every reference was checked individually
against full text or publisher metadata (Crossref, arXiv API). Treat agent-written references as unverified
until checked.}

\section{First results -- and why they were wrong}
\subsection{Validating the solver}
Before simulating coils, ElmerFEM was tested on a case with an exact answer: an infinite straight wire,
$B=\mu_0 I/2\pi r$. The first runs returned zero everywhere -- silently. Root causes: a mesh format the
converter misread, and wrong keywords in the solver input file, which Elmer ignores without an error. Starting
from Elmer's own regression test instead of hand-written input fixed it: 0.09\,\% error inside the wire, 2\,\%
outside (Fig.~\ref{fig:valid}).

\subsection{The surrogate model (report v0.1)}
Hermes believed the real coil paths were unavailable (the URLs it tried returned 404) and generated a
parametric surrogate: ellipses and bands, current calibrated to 2.5\,T. Compared with W7-X the ampere-turns per
coil matched to $0.90\times$. A report was written.
\zwei{v01_spulen_iso.png}{v01_feld_verteilung.png}{Report v0.1: the surrogate coil set and its field. Plausible
numbers -- but not a stellarator.}{fig:v01}

A topological check by the coding agent -- does each coil actually encircle the plasma axis? -- failed for 20 of
50 coils; the ``planar'' coils were horizontal bands above and below the plasma. The good match was a
coincidence. And the data had been available all along: simsopt ships the Fourier coefficients of the seven W7-X
base coils in its package data \cite{simsopt}. Two more bugs surfaced later and changed published numbers: a sign
error in the axis formula (calibration current 12.18\,kA instead of 13.49\,kA per turn), and Elmer choosing the
current direction itself, so 25 mirrored coils ran backwards (1.38\,T instead of 2.50\,T on axis). The latter is
now caught by an automated check.
\kasten{Lesson -- plausible is not correct.}{Agents are good at producing numbers that look right. Every geometry
gets an independent invariant check (winding number around the axis, clearances), and every field or force is
computed by a second, independent method before it goes into a report.}

\section{Restart: mesh directly from the data}
With the real coil paths (7 base coils, 70 by symmetry) the expected physics appeared immediately: bean-shaped
flux surfaces and the characteristic island chain (Fig.~\ref{fig:feld}). A custom FFT voxel solver matched exact
Biot-Savart to 1.3\,\% but destroyed the flux surfaces during field-line tracing, because a Cartesian grid breaks
the device's symmetry -- so field lines are always traced with Biot-Savart.
\zwei{w7x_feldlinien_3d.png}{w7x_poincare_bewertung.png}{Field lines on flux surfaces (top), Poincaré section
and rotational transform (bottom).}{fig:feld}

The plan had been to model coils in CAD. Generating the mesh directly was faster and more robust: a rectangle
at every path point, two ruled half-surfaces per coil, meshed by gmsh. Sweeps and smooth lofts failed with
self-intersecting surfaces. A clearance check on centre lines replaced boolean intersection tests (1.5\,s
instead of more than 45\,min). Elmer with all 70 coils (3.3\,M tetrahedra, 680\,s, 10\,GB) matched Biot-Savart
on the axis to a median of 0.24\,\%.
\zwei{w7x_wickelpakete.png}{elmer_70_spulen_vergleich.png}{70 winding packs meshed straight from the path data
(top); FEM vs.\ Biot-Savart along a coil normal (bottom).}{fig:elmer}
\zwei{validierung_draht.png}{w7x_voxelsolver_validierung.png}{Validation: FEM on a straight wire (top), FFT
voxel solver vs.\ Biot-Savart (bottom).}{fig:valid}

A side study ran the pipeline backwards from a target of 1\,GW net electric power: plasma size and field
$\to$ own plasma shape (DESC) $\to$ coils (simsopt). With high-temperature superconductors the device ends up at
about 10\,m major radius with forces of roughly 1\,GN per coil -- about a thousand times W7-X. From here on, the
support structure is the real problem.
\zwei{uebersicht_1GW_A6_A45.png}{struktur_A6_HTS.png}{1\,GW sizing study (top) and a first support shell with
coupled field $\to$ force $\to$ stress analysis (bottom).}{fig:gw}

\section{The parametric CAD branch}
A continuous support shell could not be constructed cleanly. The human proposed a technique from commercial CAD
(Creo's \emph{Variable Section Sweep}): per coil a closed guide curve as the casing's inner contour, sketches
normal to it, a loft through them; all walls grow outwards. The agent implemented it in headless FreeCAD as a
fully parametric model: spreadsheet parameters, sketch dimensions as expressions, symmetric coils as clones,
connectors between neighbours.
\zwei{freecad_gui_w7x.png}{gehaeuse_A45_HTS_verbunden.png}{Parametric FreeCAD model: W7-X-like casings with
connectors (top), compact 1\,GW design (bottom).}{fig:fc}

What went wrong:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Estimated, not designed, walls.} The first coupled run worked but gave stresses up to eight times
  the allowable value.
\item \textbf{Wedges break the mesher.} Inner wedges (after \cite{bachmann2024}) produced a valid CAD solid, but
  gmsh failed with ``overlapping facets'': several boolean cuts on the same winding pack created coincident
  faces. Six attempts later (tolerances, fusing in FreeCAD, deliberate overlap; the last one hung gmsh for over an
  hour) the wedge was disabled.
\item \textbf{Collisions.} A loft bulges between sketch stations and corners of tilted neighbours overlap. An
  incremental assembly check (add coils one by one, count collisions, render a video) found about 100.
\item \textbf{Fix in CAD (human idea): subtract the neighbours.} Each casing minus its neighbours' envelopes plus
  a gap: 0 collisions in all 70 steps. Meshing the whole assembly stayed fragile.
\end{enumerate}
\zwei{struktur_A45_HTS_gehaeuse.png}{gehaeuse_A45_HTS_keil_oben.png}{First stress run of the CAD casings (top);
casings with inner wedges -- valid in CAD, not meshable (bottom).}{fig:keil}
\kasten{Lesson -- valid is not meshable.}{The more boolean operations meet on the same faces, the more sliver
faces appear, and the mesher fails far away from where the problem was created. Keep the analysis model separate
from the design model.}

\section{The parallel branch: no CAD kernel}
The human's suggestion: \emph{``keep this morning's working approach for the analysis and run the CAD path in
parallel as an add-on.''} Each casing became two rectangular tubes on the exact coil path (outer contour and
winding-pack cavity) as separate surface meshes -- no boolean operations at all. Wall thicknesses follow from load
and available space; a disentangling step tests surface points of foreign casings (including intermediate rings
between stations) against the local cross-section and shrinks the cheapest wall: 4980 $\to$ 40 $\to$ 0 contact
points.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}\toprule
Model & steel casings ($E$ = 200\,GPa), homogenised winding packs ($E$ = 100\,GPa), linear elastic \\
Load & Lorentz force $\vec J\times\vec B$ from the magnetostatic solution in the same run \\
Supports & one clamped patch per casing (simplification; no connectors, no support ring) \\
Mesh / run time & 3.02\,M tetrahedra in 78\,s; solve 55\,min, about 14\,GB RAM \\
Force per coil & FEM 2.42\,MN vs.\ Biot-Savart 2.39\,MN (+1\,\%) \\
Casing von Mises & mean 11\,MPa, 99\,\% below 43\,MPa, max 110\,MPa; deformation below 1\,mm \\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{table}
Because of the simplified supports and walls as thin as 3\,mm in places this is \emph{not} a strength proof --
but it is a reproducible end-to-end pipeline.

\section{Takeaways for working with coding agents}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Verification is the job.} The most expensive error looked plausible. Independent checks
  (invariants, second methods, regression tests from upstream projects) caught every major bug.
\item \textbf{Silent failure is the default in scientific tools.} Wrong keywords ignored, current directions
  chosen by the solver, meshers hanging for an hour. Every pipeline stage now asserts on its output.
\item \textbf{Prefer the boring path.} Direct mesh generation from data beat a CAD kernel with booleans. Keeping a
  working path alive while exploring the elegant one saved the project.
\item \textbf{Humans supplied the decisive ideas} (research first, variable section sweep, neighbour subtraction,
  the parallel branch); agents supplied speed in implementing, testing and documenting them.
\item \textbf{Files are the shared memory.} A findings log, a handover file and skills let two agents work on one
  project across sessions and context compactions.
\item \textbf{References need checking} like any other agent output.
\end{itemize}

\section{Follow-up: cooling the plasma vessel}\label{sec:cooling}
\kasten{Status 27 Oct 2026.}{Flow computed with cold water; heat only estimated roughly. The numbers show
trends; they are not a design.}

\subsection{Task and layout}
The plasma vessel wall of the 1\,GW design is \SI{0.9}{m} thick and has a core of gyroid filaments built with
PicoGK \cite{picogk}. A \ang{10} segment (\ang{85}--\ang{95}) was studied. The assumed heat flux is
\SI{0.5}{MW/m^2} on \SI{26.7}{m^2} of plasma-facing surface, i.e.\ \SI{13.3}{MW}. With a \SI{10}{K} temperature
rise the segment needs \SI{319}{l/s} of water.

The layout grew step by step from the user's instructions:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item Water flows \emph{through the filaments}, not through holes in a perforated plate.
\item The inlet is on the inboard side of the torus, the outlet outboard.
\item The core sits between two inclined planes. In front of and behind it, the distribution and collection
  chambers taper off (Z-manifold). This shape came from a sketch the user drew on a screenshot.
\item The gyroid is graded: a \SI{130}{mm} layer with \SI{100}{mm} cells on the plasma side, behind it a dense,
  coarse gyroid as load-bearing structure.
\end{itemize}
The flow was computed with FluidX3D \cite{fluidx3d}, a lattice Boltzmann solver for GPUs
\cite{lehmann2023,lehmann2022ep,lehmann2022fp,lehmann2022iwocl,lehmann2021,lehmann2019,lehmann2022plic}. The
lattice has \SI{8}{mm} spacing and 259 million cells. Each run covers \SI{40}{s} of physical time with the LES
turbulence model and takes about \SI{15}{min}. FluidX3D has its own licence: free for non-commercial use only,
source-available, not an open-source licence. The two modified files (\texttt{setup.cpp}, \texttt{defines.hpp})
are published as an ``altered version'' with the original licence in the repository folder \texttt{fluidx3d/}.

\subsection{An error in the lattice}
In f6 to f9, two bands at $\pm\ang{52}$ of the circumference received almost no water. It looked like a design
flaw. Three hypotheses were tested and rejected in turn: layer thickness, orientation of the layer relative to
the gyroid, and inaccurate offset surfaces in PicoGK. Two intermediate findings were our own measurement errors.

The actual cause was the conversion of the water mesh into lattice cells. FluidX3D decides for each cell with a
ray test whether it is inside. On nearly horizontal faces of the very fine gyroid mesh this failed: at
$z=\SI{1.906}{m}$ a whole lattice layer became steel and blocked the band. It was found only by comparing the
lattice with the mesh layer by layer. Since f10, PicoGK writes its voxel occupancy to a file that FluidX3D reads
directly; it matches the mesh 100\,\%. The numbers of f6 to f9 are therefore distorted.
\bild{kuehl_gitterfehler_f9.png}{1.0}{Lattice error (f9): water in the plasma-side layer, unrolled over the
circumference (horizontal) and the path from inlet to outlet (vertical), colour = toroidal speed. The dark blocks
at about $\pm\ang{50}$ carry no flow because one lattice layer there was wrongly set to steel. Zoom below.
Labels in German.}

After that, the distribution could be balanced in one step. The wall of the front filaments now varies with
radius: \SI{5.8}{mm} inboard, \SI{4.9}{mm} top and bottom, \SI{5.2}{mm} outboard. The spread dropped from 36\,\%
to 8\,\%.

\subsection{Is it enough? A rough heat estimate}
The flow computation contains no heat. A one-dimensional estimate for pressurised water at \SI{300}{\celsius}
(Dittus--Boelter, steel with $k=\SI{25}{W/(m\,K)}$) shows two bottlenecks.
\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}\toprule
Quantity at \SI{0.5}{MW/m^2} & Temperature drop \\\midrule
Conduction through the inner skin, \SI{30}{mm} (as built) & \SI{600}{K} \\
Conduction through the inner skin, \SI{5}{mm} & \SI{100}{K} \\
Transfer to the water, $v=\SI{0.10}{m/s}$ (f11), without / with fin effect & \SI{415}{K} / \SI{210}{K} \\

The distribution is solved, the cooling is not yet. The inner skin is far too thick and the water on the plasma
side is too slow. In addition, on 10\,\% of the plasma side the water almost stands still (below
\SI{1}{mm/s}); hot spots would form there.

\subsection{Suggested improvements}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Thinner inner skin:} \SIrange{3}{5}{mm} instead of \SI{30}{mm}, possibly with tungsten armour. The
  gyroid core behind it carries the load.
\item \textbf{Faster flow on the plasma side:} either a thinner front layer (\SIrange{30}{40}{mm} instead of
  \SI{130}{mm}) with a finer cell, or a separate first-wall circuit with parallel channels at about
  \SI{1}{m/s}. The gyroid behind then only carries load and removes volumetric heat. The pressure drop rises;
  at \SI{22}{kPa} it is still small today.
\item \textbf{Remove stagnant water:} the near-zero speeds on the plasma side are probably at the end walls and
  chamber corners. They must be located in the field; guide vanes or a more open cell there are obvious options.
\item \textbf{Balance less sensitively:} one millimetre of wall thickness changes the flow by roughly a factor
  of 3.7. With typical manufacturing tolerances of $\pm\SI{0.1}{mm}$ that is about $\pm13\,\%$. Defined orifices
  at the chambers, or a larger cell with thicker walls, are more robust.
\item \textbf{Compute the heat:} coupled flow and conduction (FluidX3D has a temperature extension, or Elmer for
  the steel). Only then can the fin effect of the filaments be judged.
\item \textbf{Check the lattice:} the front filament walls are about \SI{10}{mm} thick and the lattice is
  \SI{8}{mm}. A grid study at \SIrange{4}{6}{mm} is needed before the numbers are reliable.
\item \textbf{Reach the target flow:} \SI{288}{l/s} instead of \SI{319}{l/s}. The cause is the inlet boundary
  condition, not the geometry.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Collaboration}
Here too, the decisive design ideas came from the user: water through the filaments, inlet inboard, inclined
chamber boundaries. To agree on shape, a sketch on a screenshot worked best. This led to a small tool for
drawing directly on the 3D model and handing the lines to the AI as 3D points \cite{sketch}. The lattice error
was found by the assistant, but only after several wrong guesses. The lesson: investigate an odd result before
changing the design. The workflow is described in \texttt{docs/kuehlsegment\_prozess.md} (German).

\section{Follow-up: double filament, clearance and liquid lead}\label{sec:filament}
Of the improvements suggested in Section~\ref{sec:cooling}, the second one was taken up: the plasma side gets
its own cooling circuit with fine channels. The requirements came from the user: the water should not get hotter
than \SI{150}{\celsius}, with an inlet of about \SI{60}{\celsius}. On top of that came the idea of building the
wall as a \emph{double filament}: an inner part for cooling and, separate from it, an outer part for stiffness.
All of it without colliding with the coils. All numbers in this section are rough estimates.

\subsection{Wall layout}
From the plasma side outwards: inner skin \SI{5}{mm}, cooling channels, back wall \SI{5}{mm}, dry gyroid for
stiffness, outer skin \SI{40}{mm}. The channels run toroidally across the segment from the inlet to the outlet
plenum. The gyroid no longer carries water, so it may be more open and no longer has to be tuned to the cooling
network.

\subsection{Liquid lead instead of water?}
As a next step the user proposed a \emph{gyroid within a gyroid}: cooled near the plasma with liquid lead, which
passes its heat on to the water. A gyroid wall does split space into two separate, intertwined networks and would
make a compact heat exchanger. Lead needs no pressure, and lead-lithium would also breed tritium. Three points
speak against putting the exchanger into the wall, however:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item Lead melts at \SI{327}{\celsius}, lead-lithium at about \SI{235}{\celsius}. Next to water at
  \SIrange{60}{150}{\celsius} it would freeze.
\item A leak between hot lead and water right at the plasma is dangerous and cannot be repaired.
\item In the magnetic field, induced currents brake the metal as soon as it flows across the field.
\end{enumerate}
For the third point the pressure drop was roughly estimated (\texttt{stellarator/blei\_kanal.py}):
$\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}x = \sigma\,v\,B_\perp^2\,k_p$ with $k_p = c/(1+c)$ for conducting steel walls
($c$ wall conductance ratio) and $k_p \approx 1/\mathrm{Ha}$ for an insulating layer. The field of a stellarator is
mainly toroidal, so toroidal channels run almost along the field; the pitch of the field lines was estimated at
about \ang{6}.

\subsection{Ranking by pairwise comparison}
Each variant yields several figures of merit that often conflict: f12 beats f11 on temperature and pressure drop
but loses on spread. The user suggested deciding how to proceed by pairwise comparison so that resources are used
well. Implemented in \texttt{stellarator/varianten\_vergleich.py}:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Pareto front:} a variant drops out if another one is worse in no criterion and better in at least
  one. No weights are needed.
\item \textbf{Weights by AHP} (Analytic Hierarchy Process): the user compares the criteria in pairs on a scale of
  1 to 9. From the comparison matrix $A$ with $a_{ji}=1/a_{ij}$ the weight vector $w$ is the eigenvector of the
  largest eigenvalue $\lambda_\text{max}$. Consistency is checked by
  $\mathrm{CR} = \frac{(\lambda_\text{max}-n)/(n-1)}{\mathrm{RI}_n}$ with the random index $\mathrm{RI}_4 = 0.90$;
  below 0.1 the judgements count as consistent.
\item \textbf{Rank:} weighted sum of the figures of merit normalised to 0 to 1.
\end{enumerate}
The user's judgements: temperature is five times as important as spread, pressure drop and stagnant water each;
pressure drop is three times as important as spread and five times as important as stagnant water. This gives
weights of 0.61 for temperature, 0.23 for pressure drop, 0.08 for spread and 0.08 for stagnant water, with
$\mathrm{CR} = 0.10$, right at the limit.
\bild{varianten_vergleich.png}{1.0}{Left: variants by temperature rise and spread, colour pressure drop, red
outline the Pareto front. Right: ranking with the user's weights.}
Ranking: f12 (0.73) ahead of f5 (0.69), f2 (0.58), f11 (0.42), f10 (0.38) and f1 (0.30). Notably, f11 had been
optimised for an even distribution, which the user in the end weighted low. The pairwise comparison thus showed
that effort had gone into a secondary goal.

\subsection{Does the thin skin hold the pressure?}
A thin skin is good thermally but has to carry the coolant pressure. \texttt{stellarator/haut\_festigkeit.py}
computes with Elmer a steel plate of thickness $t$ over $2\times2$ gyroid cells ($a = \SI{100}{mm}$). It is
clamped where the \SI{10}{mm} gyroid walls touch the skin. In the plane of the skin these are the points with
\[ \frac{|f|}{|\nabla f|}\,\frac{a}{2\pi} < \frac{w}{2},\qquad f = \sin X\cos Y + \sin Y\cos Z + \sin Z\cos X,\quad X = 2\pi x/a. \]
In between, the coolant pushes towards the plasma. As a check, a strip with straight supports at a spacing of
$b = a/2 - w = \SI{40}{mm}$ was computed. With both edges clamped, $\sigma = p\,b^2/(2t^2)$: at $t = \SI{5}{mm}$
Elmer gives \SI{34.3}{MPa} per MPa of pressure, the formula \SI{32.0}{MPa}.

these lines. Possible remedies: fillets at the joints, a gyroid that thickens towards the skin, or a layer of ribs
as a transition. Not yet calculated.

\subsection{Decision: pressurised water as in a nuclear plant}
With an existing turbine from a pressurised water reactor the steam conditions are fixed, and with them the
primary circuit: pressurised water at about \SI{289}{\celsius} $\rightarrow$ \SI{319}{\celsius} and \SI{16}{MPa}
as in the VVER-1000. Steam generators, main coolant pumps and pressuriser are off-the-shelf. Lead (freezing at
\SI{327}{\celsius}, braking in the magnetic field) and helium (\SI{8}{MPa}, blowers) are dropped. The first-wall
channels ($6\times\SI{6}{mm}$, three passes) were recomputed for these conditions: steel at most
\SI{441}{\celsius}, channel wall \SI{340}{\celsius} (boiling at \SI{345}{\celsius}), pressure drop \SI{0.24}{MPa}.
The steel thus sits in the working range of EUROFER (about \SIrange{300}{550}{\celsius}); with water at
\SIrange{60}{150}{\celsius} it was below it. The margin to boiling, \SI{5}{K}, is tight.

\subsection{Derivation of the heat balance}
The chain runs backwards from the grid to the plasma, with the same assumptions as the power balance of stage~0
(\texttt{invers/stufe0\_bilanz.py}), here split by component (\texttt{stellarator/waermebilanz.py}).

\paragraph{1. Thermal power from grid power.} $P_\text{grid}$ goes to the grid; the turbine converts the heat
$P_\text{th}$ into electricity with efficiency $\eta$. Pumps ($f_p P_\text{th}$) and the remaining house load
$P_\text{house}$ (coil refrigeration, tritium plant, buildings) are subtracted:
\[ P_\text{grid} = \eta P_\text{th} - f_p P_\text{th} - P_\text{house}
   \quad\Rightarrow\quad P_\text{th} = \frac{P_\text{grid} + P_\text{house}}{\eta - f_p}
   = \frac{1000 + 60}{0.35 - 0.04}\,\text{MW} = \SI{3419}{MW}. \]

\paragraph{2. Fusion power.} One D-T fusion releases \SI{17.59}{MeV}; the alpha particle carries \SI{3.52}{MeV},
the neutron \SI{14.07}{MeV}. The alpha fraction is $f_\alpha = 3.52/17.59 = 0.200$. Nuclear reactions in the
blanket multiply the neutron energy by $M = 1.15$:
\[ P_\text{th} = P_\text{fus}\,\bigl[(1-f_\alpha)M + f_\alpha\bigr] = 1.120\,P_\text{fus}
   \quad\Rightarrow\quad P_\text{fus} = \SI{3053}{MW}. \]

\paragraph{3. Split by component.} The neutrons pass through the first wall and deposit their energy in the
blanket. The alpha power stays in the plasma and leaves it as radiation (onto the whole first wall) or as particle
flow along the field lines (onto the divertor). Half each way is assumed ($f_\text{div} = 0.5$):
\begin{align*}
P_\text{blanket}  &= (1-f_\alpha)\,M\,P_\text{fus} = \SI{2808}{MW},\\
P_\text{first wall} &= (1-f_\text{div})\,f_\alpha\,P_\text{fus} = \SI{305}{MW},\qquad
q_\text{first wall} = P_\text{first wall}/S = \SI{305}{MW}/\SI{1230}{m^2} = \SI{0.25}{MW/m^2},\\
P_\text{divertor} &= f_\text{div}\,f_\alpha\,P_\text{fus} = \SI{305}{MW},\qquad
A_\text{divertor} \ge P_\text{divertor}/q_\text{max} = \SI{305}{MW}/\SI{10}{MW/m^2} = \SI{31}{m^2}.
\end{align*}
$S$ is the plasma surface of the HTS design (from stage~0: neutron wall load \SI{1.99}{MW/m^2}). The first-wall
channels are designed for \SI{0.5}{MW/m^2}, a factor of 2 margin for local peaks. \SI{10}{MW/m^2} is the design
load of the water-cooled divertor elements of Wendelstein~7-X (Wikipedia).

\paragraph{4. Cooling water.} With $\Delta T = \SI{30}{K}$ and $c_p = \SI{5380}{J/(kg\,K)}$, $\rho = \SI{732}{kg/m^3}$
at about \SI{300}{\celsius}:
\[ \dot m = \frac{P}{c_p\,\Delta T}:\qquad \dot m_\text{blanket} = \SI{17400}{kg/s}\;(\SI{23.8}{m^3/s}),\qquad
   \dot m_\text{first wall} = \SI{1890}{kg/s}\;(\SI{2.6}{m^3/s}). \]
The pumping power for the first-wall channels alone is small: $\dot V\,\Delta p/\eta_\text{pump} =
\SI{26.4}{m^3/s}\cdot\SI{0.24}{MPa}/0.8 \approx \SI{8}{MW}$, even if all the water passed through such channels.
The assumed $f_p P_\text{th} = \SI{137}{MW}$ leaves room for steam generators, piping and blanket.

\paragraph{5. Turbine.} $P_\text{gross} = \eta\,P_\text{th} = \SI{1197}{MW}$, waste heat at the condenser
$(1-\eta)P_\text{th} = \SI{2223}{MW}$. Comparison with sourced plants:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Isar~2 (Konvoi):} \SI{3950}{MW} thermal, \SI{1485}{MW} gross, \SI{1410}{MW} net, i.e.\
  $\eta_\text{gross} = 0.376$; shut down since 15~April 2023. The fusion plant would need 87\,\% of its heat and
  81\,\% of its turbine output: \textbf{one turbine of this class is enough}.
\item \textbf{VVER-1000:} \SI{3000}{MW} thermal, net efficiency 31.7\,\%, about \SI{1}{GW} electric. The fusion plant
  delivers 114\,\% of this heat; a single turbine of this size would be too small.
\end{itemize}

pressurised water reactor (labels in German).}

\subsection{Do the pipes fit through the gaps between the coils?}
All the cooling water has to go from the vessel through the coils to the steam generators and back. On the
inboard side the winding packs of the HTS design leave only \SI{0.58}{m} (stage~3). This question could rule out
the pressurised-water solution, so it was checked before anything else (\texttt{stellarator/rohr\_durchfuehrung.py}).

\paragraph{Demand.} From the heat balance: $\dot V = (\dot m_\text{blanket} + \dot m_\text{first wall})/\rho =
\SI{19290}{kg/s}\,/\,\SI{732}{kg/m^3} = \SI{26.4}{m^3/s}$ in each direction, so twice that pipe area for supply
and return. With velocity $v$ and inner diameter $d_i$ one needs
\[ n = \frac{2\,\dot V}{v\,\pi d_i^2/4}\quad\text{pipes, e.g. } n = \frac{2\cdot 26.4}{10\cdot\pi\cdot 0.8^2/4} = 11
   \text{ at } d_i = \SI{0.8}{m},\ v = \SI{10}{m/s}. \]
The pipe wall follows from the boiler formula $s = p\,d_i/(2\sigma)$: at \SI{16}{MPa} and \SI{150}{MPa},
$s = 0.053\,d_i$, so the outer diameter is $D = d_i(1 + p/\sigma) = 1.11\,d_i$.

\paragraph{Space.} From each point of the vessel's outer surface (plasma boundary plus \SI{1.05}{m}) a straight pipe
runs \SI{4}{m} outward along the surface normal. The smallest distance $a_\text{min}$ of this path to winding pack,
potting, casing and connectors of the surrogate model (R$_0$ = \SI{10.4}{m}) limits the pipe diameter:
\[ D_\text{max} = 2\,(a_\text{min} - a_\text{shield}),\qquad a_\text{shield} = \SI{0.15}{m}. \]
$a_\text{shield}$ is room for a gap and a thermal shield: the pipe is at \SI{300}{\celsius}, the superconducting
coils are cryogenic. Pipes must be at least $D + \SI{0.2}{m}$ apart; counting was greedy, freest spots first.

\paragraph{Consequences.}

\item \textbf{The choice of pressurised water is confirmed.} DEMO uses practically the same conditions for WCLL as a

\item \textbf{Lead returns, but as breeder, not as coolant.} In WCLL the PbLi flows only slowly through the blanket

  \SI{2}{MW/m^2}. That means roughly twice the heat density in the blanket and twice the radiation damage to the
  steel, hence a shorter life of the blanket modules, and more shielding in front of the coils. The
  Nb\textsubscript{3}Sn design would be at \SI{0.59}{MW/m^2}, but is almost twice as large.
\item \textbf{The tritium breeding ratio is tight.} Even DEMO misses the target of 1.15 slightly. A stellarator has
  more openings and a more complex shape, losing blanket area. This is a risk of its own and would need a neutronics
  calculation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Graded structure: flat contact at the skin, branching outward}
The user suggested solving the line-contact problem differently: ``On the blanket side one would need a flat
support, then it becomes a balancing act. On the one hand the metal strands must not be so dense that they obstruct
the water too much, on the other hand they must be thick enough for heat conduction. Further out the material can
branch heavily and become thin.'' Three design rules follow (pressurised water $p = \SI{16}{MPa}$, skin
$t = \SI{5}{mm}$, allowable $\sigma = \SI{150}{MPa}$):

\paragraph{1. Support spacing at the skin.} With the factor 1.22 from the Elmer calculation over the gyroid
(\SI{39.2}{} instead of \SI{32.0}{MPa} per MPa):
\[ \sigma = 1.22\,\frac{p\,b^2}{2t^2} \le \sigma_\text{allow}\quad\Rightarrow\quad
   b \le t\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_\text{allow}}{1.22\,p}} = \SI{5}{mm}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{300}{19.5}} \approx \SI{20}{mm}. \]
At \SI{4}{MPa} it would be \SI{39}{mm}. With supports every \SI{20}{mm} the thin skin holds pressurised water as in a
nuclear plant.

\paragraph{2. How deep the strands conduct heat.} A strand of thickness $d$ in water acts as a cooling fin with
$m = \sqrt{4h/(k\,d)}$; roughly the length $2/m$ is effective (steel $k = \SI{25}{W/(m\,K)}$):
\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{rrr}\toprule
$d$ & $h = \SI{1200}{W/(m^2K)}$ (slow) & $h = \SI{10000}{W/(m^2K)}$ (fast)\\\midrule
\SI{3}{mm} & \SI{8}{mm} & \SI{3}{mm}\\
\SI{10}{mm} & \SI{14}{mm} & \SI{5}{mm}\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\caption{Effective fin depth $2/m$.}\end{table}
The heat is in the water within \SIrange{3}{15}{mm}. Only this first layer needs thick strands; behind it the strands
only carry load.

\paragraph{3. How thin it may get further out.} The pressure pushes inner and outer skin apart; the strands hold them
together in tension. Their area fraction $\varphi$ must everywhere satisfy
\[ \varphi \ge \frac{p}{\sigma_\text{allow}} = \frac{16}{150} \approx 11\,\%. \]
Branching is therefore allowed as long as the sum of the cross-sections remains. For a gyroid of cell $a$ and wall
thickness $t_g$, $\varphi \approx 3.09\,t_g/a$.

\paragraph{Implementation in PicoGK.} \texttt{picogk/GradientWand}: \SI{5}{mm} skin, then four layers whose cell
doubles from layer to layer. Between two layers the distance fields of the gyroids with cell $a$ and $2a$ are blended
smoothly, so the walls branch. The joints are filleted with \SI{1.5}{mm}.
\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}\toprule
Layer & Cell & Wall & to depth & Steel fraction\\\midrule
0 & \SI{20}{mm} & \SI{2.5}{mm} & \SI{30}{mm} & 39\,\%\\
1 & \SI{40}{mm} & \SI{3}{mm} & \SI{80}{mm} & 23\,\%\\
2 & \SI{80}{mm} & \SI{4.5}{mm} & \SI{180}{mm} & 17\,\%\\
3 & \SI{160}{mm} & \SI{8}{mm} & \SI{570}{mm} & 15\,\%\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\caption{Layers of wall piece g1 ($240\times240\times\SI{600}{mm}$, voxel \SI{1}{mm}); 84\,\% water overall.}\end{table}
\bild{gradientwand_gesamt.jpg}{1.0}{Graded wall g1 seen from the plasma side, one quarter cut away: small cells at the
skin at the bottom, the cell doubles with each layer further out.}
\bild{gradientwand_haut_nah.jpg}{1.0}{Close-up of the skin with a window: the fine layer supports the skin almost over

Still open are the flow test (FluidX3D: does the water pass through the dense skin layer or bypass it further out?)
and the strength test (Elmer: skin and branches at \SI{16}{MPa}). The wall piece also writes out the water volume for
this.
