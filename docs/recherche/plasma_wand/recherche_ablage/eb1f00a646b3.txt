    Abstract

    A vacuum vessel in between the hot plasma and the magnetic coils is crucial for
    a fusion reactor. In this work, a tool is presented to perform the initial design
    of such a vessel automatically. Starting from the last closed flux surface, an
    algorithm is developed which lofts this manifold out as much as possible. At
    the same time, the algorithm makes sure that no intersection with the coils takes
    place. In the presented work, this algorithm is applied to the HSX stellarator
    and it has been verified that the resulting vessel wall can be built from an
    engineering perspective. Using field line tracing, the strikepoints on the vessel
    wall are examined indicating the initial locations for a divertor.
    Keywords: lofting, vacuum vessel, HSX

2      To operate a fusion device, a vacuum vessel is crucial to place in between
3   the hot plasma and the magnetic coils. In this work, a tool is presented which
4   provides the starting point for a vessel design automatically. By using the last
5   closed flux surface (LCFS) as a reference, the vessel wall is lofted outwards as
6   much as possible and at the same time it is verified that no intersection with

 8   which allows good access for blankets, heating, fueling, diagnostics, and exhaust.
 9   Maximizing the usable volume sometimes competes with the restriction that
10   the complex shapes need to be manufactured and assembled in a cost-effective

13      Vessel design is determined by the location where the plasma-wall interac-
14   tions (PWI) take place. In tokamaks and stellarators, the main PWI happens
15   at the divertor or limiter. For W7-X it has been shown that field line tracing
16   is a powerful tool to determine these locations [1]. When the location of the
17   PWI is determined, the vessel should be built in such a way that it surrounds
18   the location from the PWI and that there is sufficient space to put a divertor
19   or limiter. In ref. [2], the resiliency of an HSX wall with respect to changes
20   in the magnetic equilibrium has been studied. In there, simple wall expansions
21   lofting have been employed to loft the wall uniformly some centimeters outside
22   the LCFS as HSX currently has a small edge. This is taken as a starting point
23   for further studies on vessel and divertor design.
24      The design process of the 3-D divertor requires more detailed modeling,
25   involvement of multiple engineering teams and multiple iterations for design
26   refinements. Separating the detailed design of the divertor from the vacuum
27   vessel sub-assembly can help the individual design teams move forward without
28   the risk of losing reserved design space.
29      The method presented here is a possible way to express and manipulate
30   regions of space exterior to the plasma edge like a vacuum vessel. In many
31   ways, this method is a generalization of the precursor method for divertor design
32   described in ref. [1].
33      For the proposed method, connection length (Lc ) maps play an important
34   role as longer connection lengths agree with the locations of heat and particle
35   flux wall deposition [3] where locations with smaller connection lengths do not
36   undergo PWI and allow space for other components inside the vessel. The
37   calculation of Lc in the edge also helps identify chaotic edge structures, such

50   2. Lofting

51       The procedure of lofting a toroidal surface is outlined here. The inverse
52   coordinate representation of a toroidal 2-D surface (manifold) in 3-D space and
53   its outward perpendicular directions are described in Section 2.1. Sections 2.2.1
54   - 2.2.4 present definitions of control points, intermediate points, a set of spline
55   knots, evaluations points, and the loft distance, which represent the scaffolding
56   on which the lofted surfaces are built. The loft is defined first at the control


                                                  3
57   points, and a series of simple transformations are applied to define the position of
58   the final lofted surface defined at the evaluation points. Intersection- or collision-
59   detection with coils and other obstructions is discussed in Section 2.3. A simple
60   algorithm for automating the adjustment (lofting ‘in’ or ‘out’) is introduced in
61   Section 2.4.
62      The lofted surfaces can be exported to multiple formats, including the na-
63   tive .mat format (MATLAB) and the STL-file format (STereoLithography), as
64   discussed in Section 2.5. The use of a truncated 2-D Fourier transform of the
65   manifold data is discussed in Appendix A. Section 2.6 describes the general-
66   ization of the lofting method while starting from a different manifold than the
67   LCFS.
68      For convenience, the outward normal direction of the flux surface, projected
69   into the Φ=constant plane, serves as the definition of the direction ‘away’ from
70   the surface. In addition to rapid extrapolation of toroidal manifolds and interfer-
71   ence checks with obstructions, these approximations enable direct compatibility
72   with the input generators for the state-of-the art 3-D stellarator edge modeling
73   tools (inc. FLARE [6], EMC3-Lite [7], and EMC3-EIRENE [8]), making this
74   lofting method well-suited to be part of the the toolkit for studying edge-physics
75   models of divertor and wall components.

80   2.2. Control Points, Intermediate Points, Evaluation Points and Defining the
81        Lofted Surface

82      The method uses a set of scalar loft values and several 2-D grids (poloidal
83   by toroidal) associated with some boundary shape with periodic boundary con-
84   ditions in the toroidal and poloidal direction. Typically, the LCFS or an outer
85   surface of the plasma is chosen as the boundary. The first grid is a coarse mesh
86   of control points with an associated set of loft-values. The second grid is the
87   set of intermediate points which shares the same poloidal grid step-size as the
88   control mesh, and (typically, but not necessarily) a higher toroidal-mesh density.
89   The loft values associated with the intermediate mesh are defined by interpola-
90   tion between the straddling control points in the toroidal direction. The third
91   grid, the highest resolution one, is the set of evaluation points. The values of the
92   loft at evaluation points are based on spline curves defined by the intermediate
93   mesh along Φ=constant contours.

116   2.2.4. Defining the Lofted Surface
117       Three sets of loft values, LCP (i, j), LIP (i′ , j ′ ), and LEP (i′′ , j ′′ ) are associated
118   with the elements of SCP (i, j), SIP (i′ , j ′ ), and SEP (i′′ , j ′′ ) respectively. The
119   scalar value of each LCP (i, j) is considered ‘known’, e.g. chosen by the user or
120   generated by some algorithm. The elements of LIP (i′ , j ′ ) and LEP (i′′ , j ′′ ) are
121   constructed in two steps, or transformations described here.
122       The first transformation performs a linear interpolation of LCP (θi , Φj ) in
123   the Φ-direction (at constant θ) to generate LIP (θ, j ′ ) at each j ′ .
124       The second transformation involves generating spline curves with knots de-
125   fined by elements of LIP along constant-Φ contours. Each pair of (θi′ , LIP (i′ , j ′ ))
126   that is defined at a single toroidal angle specifies a ‘knot’ point for a spline-fit
127   displacement curve, D(θ)j ′ parameterized along the poloidal direction θ. The
128   spline curve is designed to be continuous and to have continuous 1st-derivatives
129   w.r.t. the poloidal angle. These knot points correspond to the large magenta
130   and large blue points in Figure 2 and Figure 3 (bottom row) and to the large
131   black points in the top row of Figure 3. The values of LEP (i′′ , j ′′ ) are deter-
132   mined by evaluating the appropriate displacement curve at θi′′ , D(θ = θi′′ )j ′′
133   (i.e. Figure 3, top row).
134       Regardless of the underlying (stellarator) symmetry of the magnetic config-
135   uration, up-down symmetry of the LCP (and hence, LIP and LEP ) is enforced
136   at the limits of the toroidal domain, Φ = [0, Φmax ]. The intent here is to allow
137   for some form of automated fitting/smoothing in the poloidal direction with the
138   grid size in the toroidal direction selected by the user to be consistent with the
139   spatial constraints of the specific problem on hand.
140       The set of vectors directed in the outward direction at the location of each
141   element of SEP (i′′ , j ′′ ), denoted as N̂EP (i′′ , j ′′ ), is given by Equation 12. Recall
142   that this vector lies within the plane at Φ = Φa (orthogonal to ∇Φ|Φ=Φa ).

169   2.3. Interference points: coils and other obstructions

170      For the goals of this work, the lofted surface will be adjusted to achieve
171   various goals. Two different surfaces are desired:

172      • Find the largest reasonable definition of a vacuum vessel exterior manifold
173         that encloses the largest volume possible, avoids collisions with the exter-
174         nal coils (or some proxy), and has minimal sharp excursions or localized
175         sharp features.



                                              11
176      • Find the minimal enclosing manifold that surrounds the plasma and the
177         nearby scrape-off-layer/edge region that surrounds the plasma.

178      The repeated calculation of interference checks with ‘static’ objects such as
179   coil sets, support structures, or other closely related models, will be facilitated
180   with a set of interference points IIP for each obstacle at each toroidal angle
181   in the mesh of intermediate points, SIP . An example is shown in Figure 4.
182   Here, the corners of the copper coil conductors are shown as filamentary black
183   lines. A subset of SIP is shown as small blue and magenta points on the left
184   plot. The intersection of the coils filaments with the toroidal planes-of-interest
185   are shown with blue and magenta ‘+’ symbols on both plots and form the set
186   of IIP,Coil . The blue points are associated with the SCP and SIP , while the
187   magenta points are associated only with SIP . A tolerance is included in order
188   to capture intersections/projections with filaments that are defined to extend
189   vertically and would otherwise not intersect / lie at the same toroidal angle as
190   one defined in the set of evaluation points.
191      An ‘interference check’ between the lofted surface and other external ob-
192   structions (i.e. coils, scrape-off-layer) is achieved by first constructing a set of
193   polygons, one for each toroidal angle of SLof t . Then, the winding number is
194   calculated for each of the points in IIP at each particular toroidal angle. Points
195   whose winding number is ‘0’ lie outside of the polygon at that toroidal angle
196   (and outside of the surface). Points with winding number ‘1’ lie within the
197   polygon and are enclosed by the lofted surface. The method outlined above
198   can be extended to identify intersections between different toroidal manifolds.
199   The intersections at individual toroidal planes manifest as points or poloidal
200   contours at Φ = constant cross-sections. Rapid methods exist for calculating
201   the winding number. For speed, fast in-plane interference checks are coupled
202   with fast in-plane offset calculations. While a lofting algorithm that uses N
                                                                                  ⃗ is

203   more accurate, it is also computationally more expensive.

218   2.4. Auto-lofting: The Algorithm Loop

219       Beginning with some initial guess, LCP,init (i, j), the values of LCP (i, j) can
220   be adjusted by an optimizer and collisions (or collision avoidance) with interfer-
221   ence points IIP can be used as a proxy metric to define manifolds that surround
222   or exclude certain regions of space. A simple algorithm for a step-out/step-in
223   search algorithm is shown in figure 6. A set of nested-for loops provide the
224   means to ‘scan’ over the entire array of control points. The algorithm utilizes a
225   mask array, MAdj (i, j), as an array of of True/False logic flags to indicate if the
226   corresponding control point should still be included in the attempt to step-out
227   (or in). If it is enabled, then the LCP (i, j) is adjusted by a small distance, ∆E .
228   SLof t is re-calculated and interference checks with IIP are performed. Here,
229   interference with coils is usually a feature to avoid, while enclosing all of the
230   collisions with diffusing field lines is a favorable characteristic. If an undesired
231   collision is detected, the point LCP (i, j) is moved back, SLof t is updated, and
232   the the value of MAdj (i, j) is set to False. The scan is continued/repeated until
233   there are no eligible points to move. After the scan is complete, an optional
234   ‘post-autoloft’ adjustment (not shown in the flowchart) can be applied to LCP
235   to define nested toroidal manifolds of nearly uniform thickness.
236       The value of ∆e is kept constant during the auto-lofting procedure. After
237   the loop has finished, ∆e can be reduced and the auto-loft can be repeated to

248   3. Surface Utility & Examples

249      The base configuration considered here is the so-called quasi-helically sym-
250   metric (QHS) configuration of the HSX stellarator, a 4 field period device with
251   6 unique non-planar coils per half-field period. This QHS magnetic configura-
252   tion is realized when each of the main non-planar coils are powered with equal
253   current, IM 1 = IM 2 = IM 3 = IM 4 = IM 5 = IM 6 . A line-filament model of the
254   corners of the copper conductors is used later in this section. Further details of
255   HSX can be found in Reference [13].


256   3.1. First wall

257      The first example is a lofted surface that is 3 cm away from the last closed
258   flux surface of the QHS configuration, Figure 7. This surface is very similar to
259   the existing vacuum vessel and is very ‘compact’, i.e. is has abundant space
260   (clearance) between the vessel and the coilset for assembly. However, very little
261   room is available for a divertor, and the wall acts essentially as a limiter with
262   large plasma-surface interaction regions close to the LCFS.
263      Starting from a uniform lofted value of LCP = 7 cm, a surface was auto-
264   lofted (see Section 2.4 and Figure 6 to within 1 cm of the set of interference
265   points based on the line-filament model of the corners of the copper conductors.
266   Subsequently, the loft was reduced by 2.54 cm (LCP ← LCP −2.54cm) to further
267   increase the space between the coils and the surface. Without this reduction, the
268   combined assembly of the coils with the vacuum vessel would require multiple
269   vessel sections within a half-field-period to be joined either by welds or flanges,
270   increasing the overall complexity of the vacuum vessel design and assembly
271   strategy significantly. A similar surface is constructed if the auto-loft algorithm
272   uses a loft step size of 3.54 cm with no post-autoloft step (=0). The differences

325   4. Discussion and Summary

326       This work has presented a simple method to loft a 3-D toroidal manifold in
327   a direction perpendicularly outward/inward to its surface. A series of transfor-
328   mations convert the lofting values defined at control points first onto a set of
329   intermediate points and then onto the evaluation points defined on planes of
330   constant toroidal angle. While not strictly necessary, the choice of working at
331   constant Φ-planes allows for rapid calculation of the lofted surface.
332       A set of interference points for coils and diffusing field lines proved to be
333   useful to pre-generate as they provide points in space to exclude or enclose.
334   An outline was shown for a simple automated routine to find maximal/minimal

335   volumes enclosed by lofted surface that include or exclude certain points in
336   space. Exporting the surface data in STL format and as a 2-D Fourier series
337   is possible, and multiple-stage lofted surfaces allow for very flexible manifold
338   definitions. Two general cases, a quasi-volume-maximizing ‘Vacuum Vessel’ and
339   a quasi-volume-minimizing ‘Edge Manifold’ are discussed.
340       Automating portions of the vacuum vessel design, especially at early stages,
341   can be useful for rapid virtual prototyping, enabling the ability to generate
342   simplified-yet-relevant models of the vessel in a short amount of time with
343   minimal human intervention. Larger vessels enable increased access and space
344   for ports and input power injection routes. Diagnostic views can also be en-
345   hanced/enabled because of the increased vacuum vessel boundary. Many aspects
346   of the vessel design shape can also be applied to other components, including
347   the breeding blanket and first wall. The assembly procedure may appear to be
348   out-of-scope early in the design phase, but early availability of quality vessel
349   designs may enable certain assembly choices to be made earlier in the process.
